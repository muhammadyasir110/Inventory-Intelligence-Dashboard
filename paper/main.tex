\begin{abstract}
FMCG distributors must set order quantities and safety stocks for many products that share a procurement budget and warehouse space under uncertain demand and lead times. Optimisation can compute such policies but rarely tells a planner how good a policy is, which resource binds or how much service risk remains. Following design science research, we develop an Explainable Inventory Decision Support Framework (EIDSF) and instantiate it in a working, multi-tenant decision support system. EIDSF combines a joint order-quantity and safety-stock model with expected-shortage cost, all-units discounts and budget, warehouse and service constraints; an FMCG-adapted Artificial Bee Colony algorithm (FMCG-ABC); a Lagrangian lower bound that certifies the optimality gap of any recommended policy; and a decision artifact that reports cost drivers, binding resources, model-implied versus realised service and rule-based recommendations. In a seeded study on synthetic portfolios of 6 to 100 products at an equal evaluation budget, penalty-based genetic algorithm, particle swarm and ABC rarely returned feasible policies (0--9 of 30 runs), whereas constraint-adapted variants always did. FMCG-ABC returned the cheapest policies on the main instance, came within about 0.1\% of a certified optimum without discounts and had the lowest median gap for most further instance groups, though not for 25 products. Out of sample, model-feasible policies missed individual service targets for about half of the products; modelling the review period reduced the misses from 27 to 9 of 50 at 3.9\% higher cost. All evidence is synthetic.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Inventory management in fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) distribution is a resource-allocation problem. A distributor carries hundreds of products with uncertain demand and variable supplier lead times; each product has its own service requirement; and all products compete for a limited procurement budget and for space in a few warehouses. Setting each product's order quantity and safety stock independently, for example with the economic order quantity (EOQ) and a service-level safety stock, is transparent but ignores these shared resources and can produce a policy that the organisation cannot finance or store \citep{Silver1998,Axsater2015,Zipkin2000}.

Inventory theory provides the cost and service relationships and, for structured cases, optimality conditions \citep{HadleyWhitin1963,RosenblattRothblum1990}; computational intelligence provides population-based methods such as genetic algorithms (GA), particle swarm optimisation (PSO) and the Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) algorithm for non-convex, constrained objectives \citep{Holland1975,Kennedy1995,Karaboga2007,Talbi2009}. Neither delivers a decision on its own: a metaheuristic returns a policy with no statement of its quality, and an optimal policy that a manager cannot interpret is unlikely to be adopted or properly overridden. DSS research \citep{Shim2002,Arnott2014} and explainable AI for operational research \citep{DeBock2024,Coussement2024,Olan2024} stress that models must be embedded in the decision process and explain decisions, constraints and trade-offs.

This paper reports a design science study \citep{Hevner2004,Peffers2007,GregorHevner2013} that develops and evaluates an Explainable Inventory Decision Support Framework (EIDSF) and instantiates it in a working DSS, the Inventory Intelligence Dashboard (IID). We ask how the multi-item decision can be modelled with all costs, discounts and shared resources jointly (RQ1); how ABC should be adapted to the constrained space and which adaptations matter (RQ2); how it compares with EOQ, GA, PSO and standard ABC at an equal evaluation budget and against a certified optimum (RQ3); how robust the policies are to resources, portfolio size, parameters and out-of-sample demand (RQ4); and how optimisation output can become traceable explanations that preserve managerial authority (RQ5).

{\sloppy The contributions are: \emph{(C1)} a joint order-quantity and safety-stock model with expected-shortage cost, all-units discounts and portfolio budget and warehouse constraints that is convex for every fixed price regime (Proposition~\ref{prop:convex}), yielding a Lagrangian bound that certifies the optimality gap of any policy; \emph{(C2)} FMCG-ABC, with EOQ-informed initialisation, service-as-bound encoding, feasibility repair with feasibility-first ranking and a logarithmic multi-dimensional move, each isolated by ablation; \emph{(C3)} a seeded evaluation with an equal evaluation budget, certified and exact references, non-parametric statistics and out-of-sample replay, including the conditions under which FMCG-ABC was \emph{not} best; and \emph{(C4)} a decision artifact whose every entry is computed from the policy, the model or the replay, instantiated in a multi-tenant DSS with an accountable decision workflow. We do not claim \emph{(C5)} that planners decide better with the artifact; a user evaluation is specified as future work.\par}

The work originates in the first author's MPhil thesis, \emph{Artificial Bee Colony Optimization for FMCG Supply Chains} (CASPAM, Bahauddin Zakariya University); its 30-SKU distributor dataset is not used for results because several units and parameters are unconfirmed (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\emph{Multi-item inventory with shared resources.} Reorder-point models protect against demand during an uncertain lead time; under a normal approximation the expected shortage per cycle is the standard deviation of lead-time demand times the standard normal loss function \citep{Silver1998,Axsater2015,Nahmias2009}. When items share a budget or storage resource, the optimal policy follows from Lagrange multipliers on the shared resource \citep{HadleyWhitin1963,RosenblattRothblum1990}, and Lagrangian relaxation provides bounds when constraints are hard to handle directly \citep{Everett1963,Fisher1981}. All-units discounts make purchasing cost and budget use discontinuous, which breaks the convexity these methods rely on \citep{Zipkin2000}.

\emph{Computational intelligence.} ABC \citep{Karaboga2007,Karaboga2008} has many variants, for example multi-dimensional perturbation \citep{Akay2012}, and wide supply chain use \citep{Kaya2022,Guo2025}. For multi-item problems with shared resources, \citet{Pasandideh2013} compare a tuned GA and a memetic algorithm under storage capacity and discounts, and \citet{Tan2024} combine Monte Carlo simulation with a multi-objective grey wolf optimiser under a budget. Constraint handling is often by penalty rather than feasibility-first rules \citep{Deb2000}, and comparisons often use best runs without equal budgets or reference optima \citep{Derrac2011}.

\emph{Decision support and explainability.} DSS combine data, models and an interface to support a decision maker in a semi-structured problem \citep{Shim2002,Power2002}, and design science has become the dominant DSS research paradigm \citep{Arnott2014}. Explainable AI developed mainly for predictions, with post-hoc attribution such as LIME and SHAP \citep{Ribeiro2016,Lundberg2017,Arrieta2020}; for operational research, explanations must address the decision model and its outputs \citep{DeBock2024,Coussement2024}. In optimisation-based inventory planning the natural explanatory units are cost components, binding resources, the service gap and sensitivities. Table~\ref{tab:position} positions the study; the contribution is the integration of these elements in one evaluated artifact, not a new general-purpose metaheuristic.

\section{Research method and framework}\label{sec:method}\label{sec:framework}
We followed the design science methodology of \citet{Peffers2007}. From the problem and the DSS literature we derived six design requirements: integrate decision data with provenance (DR1); represent costs, uncertainty and shared resources jointly (DR2); return feasible policies of known quality (DR3); report service risk honestly (DR4); explain the decision in management terms (DR5); and preserve managerial authority and traceability (DR6). The evaluation is ex post and artificial in the terms of the FEDS framework \citep{Venable2016}: analytical (convexity, bounds), experimental (equal-budget comparison, ablation, sensitivity, scalability, replay) and by automated testing of the model equations and the DSS.

EIDSF organises the decision process into seven stages (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}): enterprise data (ED), decision modelling (DM), computational intelligence (CI), knowledge extraction (KE), explanation (EX), recommendation (RE) and managerial evaluation (ME). The optimised policy is an intermediate artifact that is analysed, explained and then evaluated by a manager, not the decision itself.

\section{Decision model}\label{sec:model}

We consider $N$ SKUs assigned to warehouses $w=1,\dots,W$ ($I_w$ the SKUs in warehouse $w$) over a 365-day year. SKU $i$ has mean daily demand $d_i$ with standard deviation $\sigma_{d,i}$, annual demand $D_i=365d_i$, mean lead time $L_i$ (days) with standard deviation $\sigma_{L,i}$, ordering cost $K_i$, holding cost $h_i$ per unit and year, shortage cost $c_{s,i}$ per unit short, regular price $p^{reg}_i$, discounted price $p^{disc}_i<p^{reg}_i$ for orders of at least $q^{disc}_i$ units (all-units discount), unit volume $v_i$ and target cycle service level $\alpha_i\ge0.5$; $B$ is the procurement budget and $Cap_w$ the warehouse capacity. Demand and lead time are independent, lead-time demand is approximated as normal, and shortages are backordered. The decisions are $Q_i$ and $SS_i$, with $ROP_i=d_iL_i+SS_i$. The standard deviation of lead-time demand and the model-implied service level are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sdlt}
\sdlt=\sqrt{L_i\sigma_{d,i}^2+d_i^2\sigma_{L,i}^2},\qquad z_i=SS_i/\sdlt,\qquad CSL^{\mathrm{model}}_i=\Phi(z_i).
\end{equation}
With the standard normal loss function $\mathcal{L}(z)=\phi(z)-z\,[1-\Phi(z)]$, the expected shortage per cycle is $\sdlt\mathcal{L}(z_i)$ and the annual cost is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tc}
TC(Q,SS)=\sum_{i=1}^N\Big[\underbrace{K_i\frac{D_i}{Q_i}}_{C_{o,i}}+\underbrace{h_i\Big(\frac{Q_i}{2}+SS_i\Big)}_{C_{h,i}}+\underbrace{c_{s,i}\frac{D_i}{Q_i}\sdlt\,\mathcal{L}(z_i)}_{C_{s,i}}+\underbrace{D_i\,p_i(Q_i)}_{C_{p,i}}\Big],
\end{equation}
where $p_i(Q_i)=p^{disc}_i$ if $Q_i\ge q^{disc}_i$ and $p^{reg}_i$ otherwise. Because purchasing dominates $TC$, all relative gaps are computed on the \emph{controllable cost} $\Cctrl=TC-C_0$ with the constant $C_0=\sum_i D_i\min(p^{reg}_i,p^{disc}_i)$. The model is
\begin{align}
\min_{Q,SS}\ TC(Q,SS)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad & \textstyle\sum_{i} p_i(Q_i)\,Q_i \le B, \label{eq:budget}\\
& \textstyle\sum_{i\in I_w} v_i\,(Q_i/2+SS_i)\le Cap_w,\qquad w=1,\dots,W, \label{eq:wh}\\
& SS_i\ge z_{\alpha_i}\sdlt,\quad z_{\alpha_i}=\Phi^{-1}(\alpha_i), \label{eq:service}\\
& Q^{\min}_i\le Q_i\le Q^{\max}_i,\qquad 0\le SS_i\le SS^{\max}_i. \label{eq:bounds}
\end{align}
Constraint (\ref{eq:budget}) limits the purchase value of one batch of every SKU at the active price (the budget formulation of the thesis); the average-investment form $\sum_i p_i(Q_i)(Q_i/2+SS_i)\le B$ is tested as a variant (Section~\ref{sec:res-variants}). A second variant relaxes continuous review by subtracting the expected daily-review undershoot $u_i$ from the protection, $z_i=(SS_i-u_i)/\sdlt$.

\paragraph{Convexity and a certified lower bound} Let $\bar\Phi=1-\Phi$ and $R(z)=\bar\Phi(z)/\phi(z)$ (Mills' ratio).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mills}
For every $z\ge0$, $2\mathcal{L}(z)\phi(z)-\bar\Phi(z)^2>0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Dividing by $\phi^2$, the claim is $R^2+2zR-2<0$, i.e.\ $R(z)<b(z):=\sqrt{z^2+2}-z$. With $R'=zR-1$, one finds $b'-(zb-1)=z(z\sqrt{z^2+2}-z^2-1)/\sqrt{z^2+2}\le0$ for $z\ge0$ (equality only at $0$), because $(z^2+1)^2-z^2(z^2+2)=1$. Hence $\Delta=b-R$ satisfies $\Delta'-z\Delta\le0$, so $e^{-z^2/2}\Delta(z)$ is non-increasing on $[0,\infty)$ with limit zero (both $R$ and $b$ are bounded); it is therefore positive, and $\Delta(z)>0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:convex}
For a fixed price regime of each SKU, model (\ref{eq:tc})--(\ref{eq:bounds}) is a convex program if $\sigma_{DLT,i}>0$, $c_{s,i}>0$, $D_i>0$ and $\alpha_i\in[0.5,1)$. The same holds for the average-investment budget and the review-period variant.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Within a regime, $C_{o,i}$ is convex, $C_{h,i}$ linear, $C_{p,i}$ constant and the constraints linear. For $g(Q,S)=c\,\mathcal{L}(S/\sigma)/Q$ with $c=c_sD\sigma>0$, using $\mathcal{L}'=-\bar\Phi$ and $\mathcal{L}''=\phi$: $g_{QQ}=2c\mathcal{L}/Q^3>0$, $g_{SS}=c\phi/(\sigma^2Q)>0$ and $\det\nabla^2g=c^2(2\mathcal{L}\phi-\bar\Phi^2)/(\sigma^2Q^4)$, which is positive on the feasible set $z\ge z_{\alpha_i}\ge0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:mills}. The variants only add a linear constraint or an affine shift of $z$.
\end{proof}

Discounts therefore make the problem non-convex only through the choice among $2^N$ price-regime combinations. Relaxing (\ref{eq:budget}) and (\ref{eq:wh}) with multipliers $\lambda\ge0$, $\mu\ge0$ gives
\begin{multline}\label{eq:dual}
g(\lambda,\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^N\min_{(Q_i,SS_i)\in X_i}\Big[TC_i+\lambda p_i(Q_i)Q_i+\mu_{w(i)}v_i\big(\tfrac{Q_i}{2}+SS_i\big)\Big]\\
-\lambda B-\sum_w\mu_wCap_w\le TC^*
\end{multline}
by weak duality \citep{Everett1963,Fisher1981}, where $X_i$ collects (\ref{eq:service})--(\ref{eq:bounds}). The relaxation separates by SKU; each SKU is solved per price regime and the cheaper kept, so the $2^N$ combinations are never enumerated. For fixed $Q_i$ the optimal safety stock has the closed form $1-\Phi(z_i^*)=(h_i+\mu_{w(i)}v_i)Q_i/(c_{s,i}D_i)$, clipped to $[z_{\alpha_i},SS^{\max}_i/\sdlt]$; by Proposition~\ref{prop:convex} the partial minimum is convex in $Q_i$ and is found by golden-section search on $\log Q_i$. We maximise $g$ by coordinate ascent; every visited point is a valid bound. Without discounts strong duality holds \citep{Boyd2004} and the bound is tight; with discounts it remains valid but a duality gap can remain. Either way, the gap to the bound is an upper bound on the true optimality gap, which the DSS reports with every recommendation.

\section{FMCG-ABC}\label{sec:abc}

All stochastic methods search $x\in[0,1]^{2N}$, which a common decoder maps to $(Q,SS)$, call the same evaluation function and stop after $E_{\max}=50{,}000$ evaluations. In standard ABC \citep{Karaboga2007}, $SN=50$ food sources are improved by employed and onlooker bees that perturb one dimension, $v_{jk}=x_{jk}+\varphi_{jk}(x_{jk}-x_{rk})$ with $\varphi_{jk}\sim U(-1,1)$, with greedy selection; a source not improved for 120 trials is replaced by a random scout; onlookers are chosen rank-proportionally. FMCG-ABC adds three groups of adaptations, evaluated cumulatively:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt]
\item[A2 EOQ-informed initialisation.] Half of the sources start around the EOQ policy ($\pm20\%$ in $Q$, $SS_i=z_{\alpha_i}\sdlt(1+U(0,0.2))$), half uniformly.
\item[A3 Constraint adaptation.] (i) The service constraint is encoded as a lower bound of $SS_i$. (ii) If (\ref{eq:budget}) or (\ref{eq:wh}) is violated, $(Q,SS)$ is moved towards its lower bounds along a segment whose parameter is found by 30 bisection steps keeping a feasible end, and the repaired policy is written back (Lamarckian repair); if even the lower-bound policy is infeasible, infeasibility is reported. (iii) Sources are ranked feasibility-first \citep{Deb2000}. Without A3, constraints are handled by the quadratic penalty $F=TC+\rho\sum_j\tilde V_j^2$ with normalised violations and $\rho=10^3\,TC(\text{EOQ})$.
\item[A4 Inventory-specific move (full FMCG-ABC).] Order quantities are decoded on a logarithmic scale, so moves are multiplicative and comparable across SKUs, and each dimension is perturbed with probability $MR=0.1$ \citep{Akay2012}.
\end{description}
The reported solution is the best feasible policy evaluated; if none is feasible the run is reported as infeasible. \emph{Comparators}: EOQ with static safety stock (all-units-discount EOQ per SKU, ignoring coupling constraints); a real-coded GA (tournament, SBX $p_c=0.9$, $\eta_c=15$, polynomial mutation, elitism, population 100) \citep{DebAgrawal1995}; and global-best PSO with constriction coefficients (swarm 100) \citep{ClercKennedy2002}. GA, PSO and ABC (A1) use the penalty. To separate the search engine from the inventory adaptations, \emph{GA+} and \emph{PSO+} receive exactly the A2--A3 adaptations. All parameters were fixed before the experiments.

\section{Evaluation design}\label{sec:design}

The experiments use a documented, seeded generator (Table~\ref{tab:gen}). Daily demand is Gamma distributed (not normal); days 1--730 calibrate $d_i$ and $\sigma_{d,i}$ and days 731--1000 are held out. The budget and capacities are fractions $\tau_B$, $\tau_C$ of what the EOQ policy would use, so the coupling constraints bind. No result is based on real company data.

\emph{E1} compares EOQ and eight stochastic methods (GA, PSO, ABC, A2, A3, FMCG-ABC, GA+, PSO+) on one 50-SKU instance ($\tau_B=0.75$, $\tau_C=0.80$; 30 runs each). \emph{E2} removes the discounts, so the bound is tight and certifies the optimum. \emph{E3} uses five 6-SKU discounted instances whose optimum is obtained by enumerating all $2^6$ price regimes, each a convex program, and certified by per-regime bounds. \emph{E4/X3} test five further 50-SKU instances at tight ($\tau=0.60$) and loose ($\tau=0.90$) resources; \emph{E5/X2} test $N\in\{25,50,75,100\}$ on three instances per size; \emph{E6/X4} re-optimise under one-at-a-time parameter changes; \emph{X1} gives GA+ and PSO+ equal wall-clock time; \emph{X5} and \emph{X6} use the average-investment budget and the review-period variant; and \emph{E7} replays the median policy of each method on the holdout. In the \emph{replay}, a fixed policy $(Q_i,ROP_i)$ is simulated with daily review and backorders, random lead times and 30 replications, recording realised cycle service level (CSL), fill rate and costs.

We report feasible runs and, over feasible runs, median and interquartile range of $\Cctrl$ and the gap $100(TC-TC^*)/(TC^*-C_0)$ to the best known solution (BKS) or the certified optimum. Because runs of different methods are independent samples, FMCG-ABC is compared with each method by two-sided Mann--Whitney U tests \citep{MannWhitney1947} with Holm correction \citep{Holm1979}, bootstrap confidence intervals of the median difference and the Vargha--Delaney effect size $A_{12}$ \citep{VarghaDelaney2000}, and a Kruskal--Wallis test \citep{KruskalWallis1952} across methods; infeasible policies are never compared on cost. All tables are generated by one command from the stored outputs.
