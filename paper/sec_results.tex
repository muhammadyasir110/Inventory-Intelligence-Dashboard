\subsection{Feasibility and cost (E1)}\label{sec:res-e1}
The EOQ policy needs 133\% of the budget and 125\% of the capacity of the more constrained warehouse; its controllable cost (16.0 million) is not attainable, and imposing the shared resources roughly doubles the controllable cost of the best feasible policies (Table~\ref{tab:e1}). The generic penalty-based methods rarely returned an admissible policy (PSO in none of 30 runs, ABC in one, GA in nine), and their feasible policies cost more than twice the BKS; their solutions violated the constraints only slightly in normalised terms, the typical outcome of a quadratic penalty. All constraint-adapted methods were feasible in every run. FMCG-ABC had the lowest median controllable cost (34.10 million, interquartile range 2.52), ahead of PSO+ (40.23), GA+ (40.30) and A3 (54.21), and found the BKS (29.71 million). The Kruskal--Wallis test rejects equal distributions ($H=83.9$, $p<10^{-4}$); the medians of FMCG-ABC were lower than those of A3, GA+ and PSO+ by 20.11, 6.20 and 6.12 million (Holm-corrected $p<10^{-4}$; $A_{12}=1.00$, 0.95 and 0.81): the advantage over PSO+ is clear but not uniform.

\subsection{Distance to the optimum (E1--E3)}\label{sec:res-gap}
The Lagrangian bound of the main instance is 25.02 million; the BKS is 18.7\% and the FMCG-ABC median 36.3\% above it. With discounts these are upper bounds on the true gaps, which E2 and E3 measure exactly (Table~\ref{tab:exact}). Without discounts (E2) the bound is tight (duality gap $10^{-6}$\%), and the adapted methods came within 0.10\% (FMCG-ABC, GA+) and 0.17\% (PSO+) of the certified optimum in median, while penalty-based PSO and ABC returned no feasible policy. On the small discounted instances (E3) the adapted methods had almost identical median gaps (PSO+ 7.6\%, GA+ 7.7\%, FMCG-ABC 7.9\%), with worst runs 47\% above the optimum; 135 of 150 runs ended in a suboptimal combination of price regimes, but within the selected regime they were a median 0.15\% from its optimum. The hardness lies in the discrete choice of price regimes, not in the continuous part.

\subsection{Which adaptation matters? (ablation)}\label{sec:res-ablation}
EOQ-informed initialisation (A2) raised the feasible runs from 1 to 21 of 30 and lowered the median feasible cost from 80.5 to 60.9 million; the constraint handling (A3) made every run feasible (54.2 million); and the log-scale multi-dimensional move (A4) produced the largest single improvement, to 34.1 million (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The same constraint handling also made GA and PSO competitive (GA+, PSO+), so it is not specific to ABC; what distinguished FMCG-ABC is its combination with the perturbation scheme.

\subsection{Computing time, robustness and scalability (X1, E4--E6)}\label{sec:res-robust}
Our ABC evaluates candidates one at a time, whereas GA and PSO are vectorised: FMCG-ABC needed about 32 seconds per run against 2 (PSO+) and 3 seconds (GA+). Given equal wall-clock time (10 to 19 times more evaluations), PSO+ and GA+ reached median controllable costs of 40.24 and 38.35 million against 34.00 million for FMCG-ABC; the difference is significant against GA+ ($p<0.001$, $A_{12}=1.00$) but not against PSO+ ($p=0.064$, $A_{12}=0.75$).

On five further 50-SKU instances, FMCG-ABC had the lowest median gap at tight (4.7\% vs.\ 10.3\% for PSO+ and 13.4\% for GA+) and loose resources (5.5\% vs.\ 14.1\% and 19.1\%), although at loose resources PSO+ attained the best solution more often (3 vs.\ 1 of 25 runs). Over three instances per size, FMCG-ABC had the lowest median gap for $N=50$, 75 and 100 (4.2\%, 5.3\% and 4.9\%) but not for $N=25$, where PSO+ led (8.3\% vs.\ 10.9\%); standard ABC was feasible only for $N=25$. Controllable cost rose by 18.5\% with 10\% less warehouse capacity, by 14.4\% with 10\% less budget and by 11.4\% with a one-point higher service target, and fell by 16.8\% with 10\% more budget; demand variability moved it by only $-3.2\%$ ($\sigma_d\times0.8$) and $+7.0\%$ ($\sigma_d\times1.2$). Binding resources and service targets drive the policy cost more than demand variability does.

\subsection{Out-of-sample service (E7)}\label{sec:replay}
All feasible policies met their targets on average in the model (mean model-implied CSL 95.0--96.4\% against a mean target of 93.1\%), but on the holdout (Table~\ref{tab:replay}) the realised CSL was 92.9\% for FMCG-ABC, 92.3\% for PSO+, 94.8\% for GA+ and 94.7\% for A3, with 25 to 28 of the 50 SKUs below their individual targets, while fill rates stayed above 99.3\%; the EOQ policy realised 90.5\% with 38 SKUs below target. Replacing Gamma by normal demand did not remove the shortfall (91.7\%, 28 SKUs below target), whereas adding the expected daily-review undershoot $(\sigma_{d,i}^2+d_i^2)/(2d_i)$ to the reorder points raised the realised CSL to 95.3\% (17 SKUs below target) but violated the capacity of the second warehouse (105.4\%). The continuous-review assumption, not demand non-normality, is the main cause, and the allowance must be optimised within the model rather than added afterwards. Model feasibility is necessary but not sufficient for service.

\subsection{Model variants (X5, X6)}\label{sec:res-variants}
With the average-investment budget (X5), all adapted methods remained feasible in every run, standard ABC in none, and FMCG-ABC again had the lowest median controllable cost (53.64 million, against 56.56 for GA+ and 63.22 for PSO+). With the daily-review undershoot built into the model (X6), the re-optimised FMCG-ABC policy cost 35.42 million, 3.9\% more than under continuous review, and raised the realised CSL from 92.9\% to 96.8\%, with 9 instead of 27 SKUs below target, while respecting the capacities; GA+ and PSO+ reached 96.5\% and 95.9\%. The variant is offered to planners as an alternative policy with its cost and service consequences, not as a replacement.
