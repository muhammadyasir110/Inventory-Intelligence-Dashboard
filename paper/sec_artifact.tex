We demonstrate the decision artifact on the median FMCG-ABC policy of the main instance; it is produced by the same code that the DSS calls.

\paragraph{Economic explanation} The artifact decomposes the model cost into its components. Compared with the (infeasible) EOQ policy, the feasible FMCG-ABC policy orders smaller quantities for 33 of the 50 SKUs, which raises ordering cost by 82\% and lowers holding cost by 13\%, and takes the quantity discount for 17 SKUs; the unconstrained optimum understates the cost of a policy the organisation can finance and store (16.0 against 34.1 million controllable). Holding is the largest controllable cost component for 30 SKUs and ordering for 20; shortage is never the largest, so a manager sees that the policy is shaped by the trade-off between order frequency and stock investment under binding resources.


\paragraph{Constraint and service explanation} The policy uses 99.9\% of the budget and 95.2\% and 99.8\% of the two warehouses. Utilisation alone does not say which limits constrain the policy: the budget and the second warehouse are at their limits with positive multipliers, whereas the first warehouse is highly used but its multiplier is zero ($\lambda=0.91$; $\mu=(0,\;17{,}924)$ per m$^3$). For a 10\% change of the budget the multipliers estimate $\pm3.73$ million, close to the recomputed change of the bound ($+3.91$ and $-3.22$ million). In the model every SKU meets its target; in the replay 27 SKUs fall short.


\paragraph{Recommendations} Table~\ref{tab:rules} lists the rules ($ESE_i=ES_i/Q_i$ is the expected fraction of annual demand short); their thresholds were fixed before the experiments and are stored with every artifact. In the first design iteration every rule was attached to SKU records and fired 155 times, including R4 and R5 for all 50 SKUs, which carried no SKU-specific information and flagged a warehouse that does not constrain the policy. In the second, implemented iteration SKU records contain only R1--R3 and R6 (55 firings) and resources are reported once at portfolio level as \emph{constraining}, \emph{high utilisation, not constraining} or \emph{not constraining}, derived from the limit and the multiplier. Table~\ref{tab:artifact} shows the record of the SKU with the most rules fired: it states each fact with the number behind it and the suggested action, and leaves the trade-off (R1 suggests more protection, R2 a smaller order quantity, under a constraining budget) to the manager.

\paragraph{Fidelity} Every number in the artifact is recomputed from $(Q,SS)$ with the model or taken from the replay, and automated tests check that cost components sum to the reported total and that each fired rule's evidence satisfies its condition. This gives a traceable recommendation $\to$ evidence $\to$ model-output chain. None of the explanations is causal.

\subsection{The Inventory Intelligence Dashboard}\label{sec:iid}
The IID (release 2.7.0) is a multi-tenant web application (FastAPI, PostgreSQL, browser frontend) that embeds the artifact in an operational planning workflow (Figure~\ref{fig:iid}). Every record belongs to an organisation; isolation between organisations and the permissions of six roles (owner, administrator, inventory, procurement and warehouse manager, read-only executive) are enforced on the server and tested for every interface operation, and sign-in can require a second factor (time-based one-time passwords). Inventory files are imported only after a validation preview that reports every derived field and records the data provenance (synthetic or customer data). Stock changes after an import (adjustments with a reason code, goods receipts and transfers between warehouses) are posted to a transaction ledger that can be reconciled against the imported quantities. Demand forecasts are produced only from recorded history; products with intermittent demand are classified by the scheme of \citet{SyntetosBoylanCroston2005} and forecast with the Syntetos--Boylan approximation \citep{SyntetosBoylan2005} of Croston's method \citep{Croston1972}, selected by holdout error. Purchase orders follow an approval workflow, and a manager accepts, modifies or rejects each recommendation, with a comment required for modifications and rejections; every decision is stored in an audit log with the user and the evidence. The release passes 299 automated tests, including tests of the model equations, cross-organisation isolation and the browser interface.

The explainable-policy view (Figure~\ref{fig:iid}b) applies the artifact to the organisation's current records: the planner selects a method and obtains the feasibility status, model cost, lower bound and certified gap, the portfolio-level resource status with shadow prices, and one row per SKU with policy, cost components, primary driver, model-implied service and the evidence and action of each fired rule; the mapping from records to model parameters is stated below the table. On the synthetic sample file shown, the budget is 96.4\% used but its shadow price is zero, so it is reported as ``high utilisation, not constraining''; under the first rule design R5 would have fired for all 64 SKUs. Rules that need a holdout replay (R1, R6) are reported as not evaluated rather than silently omitted. A public demonstration instance with the synthetic sample file is available at \url{\demourl} (access for reviewers is provided with the submission).
