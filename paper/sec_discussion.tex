\subsection{Answers to the research questions}
\emph{RQ1.} The joint model couples SKUs through the budget and the warehouses, and Proposition~\ref{prop:convex} shows that its only source of non-convexity is the choice of price regimes. The experiments confirm that this is where methods fail: without discounts the adapted methods came within 0.1--0.2\% of a certified optimum, whereas on small discounted instances most runs selected a suboptimal regime combination.
\emph{RQ2.} The adaptations that matter most are inventory-specific constraint handling, which turned a search that almost never returned a feasible policy into one that always did, and the log-scale multi-dimensional move, which reduced the median gap to the best known solution from 82\% to 15\%. The constraint handling transfers to GA and PSO, so it is a property of the problem encoding rather than of the bee-colony metaphor.
\emph{RQ3.} FMCG-ABC returned the cheapest feasible policies on the main instance at an equal evaluation budget, kept the lowest median at equal computing time (significantly against GA+ only) and had the lowest median gap for 50 to 100 SKUs and under the average-investment budget, but not for 25 SKUs, and it was practically tied with GA+ and PSO+ on small discounted instances. We claim an advantage on these synthetic instance families, not general superiority; the more robust finding is that generic penalty-based metaheuristics returned inadmissible policies under binding resources.
\emph{RQ4.} The policies remained feasible under tighter and looser resources and for larger portfolios; their cost was driven by capacity, budget and service targets more than by demand variability. Out of sample, model-feasible policies missed individual targets for about half of the SKUs, mainly because of the continuous-review assumption.
\emph{RQ5.} The artifact turns a policy into checkable statements about cost drivers, binding resources, the gap to a lower bound, the divergence of model-implied and realised service, and the evidence behind each fired rule.

\subsection{Implications}
Three design principles generalise beyond inventory. First, \emph{feasibility is a first-class output}: a DSS that ranks decisions by a penalised objective can recommend decisions that violate hard business limits; encoding non-negotiable constraints as bounds or repairs, and reporting infeasibility rather than the least-bad candidate, prevents this. Second, \emph{a heuristic recommendation should carry a certificate}: a lower bound on the same instance turns ``the algorithm found this'' into ``this policy is at most $x$\% more expensive than any feasible policy''. Third, \emph{explanations must include what the model does not know}, such as realised next to model-implied service. Unlike the closest multi-item studies \citep{Pasandideh2013,Tan2024}, the framework reports a bound on solution quality and out-of-sample service; its effect on decision quality, which the explainable-AI literature for operational research calls for \citep{DeBock2024,Coussement2024}, has not yet been measured. For FMCG planners, the results show that planning SKUs independently understates the controllable cost of a feasible policy by about half on the studied portfolio, that budget and warehouse limits drive the policy, and that service targets set in a continuous-review model should be checked against the review practice actually used.

\subsection{Limitations and future research}\label{sec:limits}
\emph{Data.} All results are synthetic. Before the thesis' 30-SKU distributor dataset can be used, the period of its demand standard deviation, the units of lead time and volume, the basis of its holding and shortage costs, the budget, the warehouse capacities and assignment, the service targets and the missing lead-time variability and discount data must be confirmed.
\emph{Model and method.} The model assumes independent normal lead-time demand, backorders and average stored volume; the review-period variant still leaves 9 SKUs below target, which a periodic-review formulation or simulation-based service constraints could address. Our ABC implementation is not vectorised, parameters were fixed in advance rather than tuned per method, and with discounts the bound is not tight, which branching over price regimes guided by the dual could improve.
\emph{Usefulness to decision makers.} The explanation layer has been evaluated for fidelity, not for its effect on decisions. A feasible next study is a within-subject experiment with 20--30 inventory planners or supply chain students who make replenishment and budget decisions on synthetic portfolios with the optimised policy alone and with the full artifact, in randomised order, measuring the cost and service of their decisions under replay, the detection of seeded errors, decision time, the overrides recorded by the IID workflow and perceived usefulness. Until then, the DSS contribution is a designed, implemented and technically evaluated artifact, not evidence of better decisions.
